Self-Rewriting Differential Evolution with Operator Synthesis

Iztok Fister Jr., Aleksander Kalacun, Iztok Fister

ACM GECCO Companion 2026, pp. 389–392

A differential evolution method that evolves its mutation operators as short symbolic programs. Successful operators are written back into the algorithm’s source code and retained across restarts, allowing the search strategy itself to adapt. We evaluated the approach against standard differential evolution on five benchmark functions.

DOI ↗
Summary (expand) for Self-Rewriting Differential Evolution with Operator Synthesis

Abstract

Although evolving algorithms are based on principles of biological evolution, such as reproduction, mutation, and selection, they still obey the von Neumann principle "Program = algorithm + data". These algorithms remain rigid in the sense that their code does not change in computer memory during execution; their evolvability mainly concerns the data they process. In this paper, we introduce a novel variant of differential evolution that can adapt at the code level, overcoming this rigidity. In our proposed method, differential evolution mutation strategies are represented as short symbolic programs. Periodically, the system synthesizes new operators by mutating the top-performing operators through variable swaps, F-scaling, and the addition of difference terms. It merges these operators into a deduplicated library with a size cap and assigns credit based on empirical success using a softmax function. After each phase, the method rewrites its own program by serializing the evolved operator library and the best solution back into its source code and re-executing it, producing heritable, cumulative changes to the program itself. On canonical benchmarks, the optimizer discovers effective, previously absent operators and improves across phases, illustrating a practical path toward structural replication and self-adaptation.

MARNN: A New Method for Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning

Aleksander Kalacun, Iztok Fister Jr.

IEEE ERK 2025 · pp. 601–604

A GraphRNN-inspired method for coordinating agents in dynamic, partially observed graph environments. It combines recurrent memory, graph convolutional observation encoders, and attention-based communication. We evaluated it against IQL, QMIX, and VDN in custom package-delivery environments, which the study also introduces as benchmarks for multi-agent reinforcement learning.

Proceedings ↗
Summary (expand) for MARNN: A New Method for Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning

Abstract

This paper presents a new method for dynamic, partially observed multi-agent Markov decision processes with stochastic elements. We use this formulation to define and simulate real-world problems and represent them as graphs. Existing multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithms struggle in changing environments that lack consistency. The core idea of the proposed algorithm is based on the deep autoregressive model GraphRNN, enabling long-term goal memorization and greater resilience to stochastic changes in the environment. Policy decisions are based on graph convolutional network encoders. Using observations and information communicated by other agents through graph attention network encoders, agents decide on the best next state. To demonstrate the method's capabilities, we implemented new graph-based environments. We used these environments to evaluate multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithms on problems resembling real-world situations, providing an effective benchmark.

Parameter-Dependent Intervention Pruning in Causal Reinforcement Learning via Tropical Geometry

Aleksander Kalacun, Iztok Fister Jr., Iztok Fister

Planned submission to IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems (TNNLS) in 2026

We investigate how tropical geometry can reduce the intervention choices considered by causal reinforcement learning agents. The method uses information about an environment’s parameters to prune suboptimal choices before a causal bandit policy selects an action.

Summary (expand) for Parameter-Dependent Intervention Pruning in Causal Reinforcement Learning via Tropical Geometry

Abstract

Causal reinforcement learning agents, such as structural causal bandit agents, must not only find a policy that maximizes reward in an uncertain environment but also decide where to intervene: which variables to act on and which to leave to their natural, possibly confounded mechanisms. Causal agents use a three-level hierarchy to interact with their environment: observational, interventional, and counterfactual. Established structural causal bandit methods, such as POMIS, reduce the complexity of searching the intervention set from O(3^k) to O(2^k), where k denotes the number of arms, by using the causal diagram to prune targets that are never optimal. However, the POMIS intervention set deliberately represents the worst case and ignores environment-specific parameter restrictions implied by the available data.

This paper addresses that gap by introducing a second, parameter-dependent intervention-pruning layer. The problem is transformed into a tropical geometry representation that incorporates the parameters into the optimal-arm decision. The proposed pipeline consists of five steps: (1) calculate the POMIS-reduced arm set from the causal graph G; (2) decompose the environment parameterization using c-factorization and identify a reward distribution; (3) tropicalize the reward distribution; (4) apply parameter-dependent tropical pruning to remove only provably suboptimal arms, certified using a linear program; and (5) choose an action using an online policy for structural causal bandits. In the pruning step, arms are represented by a tropical surrogate in which each monomial dominates the others in the observed tropical rational functions containing the reward distributions.

The proposed method is policy-agnostic and introduces two variants of structural causal bandit algorithms: the upper confidence bound method PDTP-UCB and the Thompson sampling method PDTP-TS. It applies to environments that satisfy the stated assumptions. We evaluated the approach on synthetic drug-combination benchmarks with k ∈ {3, 4, 6} arms. Across 100 independent runs with a time horizon of T = 10,000, PDTP-UCB reduced the UCB1-on-POMIS assignment-arm set from 12 arms to 2, from 36 to 4, and from 144 to 5, respectively. PDTP-UCB also reduced final cumulative regret relative to UCB1-on-POMIS by a factor of approximately 11 to 19. Replacing the online policy with Thompson sampling improved the results further.

Paper (English) ↗Book of abstracts ↗
Summary (expand) for Factors affecting the rate of reaction of benzyl alcohol oxidation with potassium permanganate in nonpolar solvent using crown ethers

Abstract

In this research, we investigated the practical use of crown ethers in the laboratory. Crown ethers are cyclic organic compounds that can bind ions of a suitable size within their central cavity. They form a nonpolar shell around a polar ion, allowing compounds normally soluble only in polar solvents to dissolve in nonpolar solvents. Reactions involving substances with different solubilities are a common challenge in laboratory research, while alternatives such as DMSO can pose environmental hazards. We therefore investigated the optimal crown ether concentration and reaction temperature.

We studied the oxidation of benzyl alcohol, which is soluble in nonpolar solvents, with potassium permanganate, which is soluble in polar solvents, using a crown ether as a catalyst and diethyl ether as the solvent. Without the crown ether, the reaction does not occur because the potassium permanganate remains solid and settles at the bottom. With the crown ether, oxidation proceeds and produces benzaldehyde.

We tested different crown ether concentrations at 30 °C, measuring the benzaldehyde concentration every five minutes for one hour with an ATR-FTIR spectrophotometer. We repeated the procedure at different temperatures while keeping the crown ether concentration constant. After comparing absolute concentration changes and areas under the concentration–time curves, we found that the highest benzaldehyde concentration occurred at 35 °C and with 0.4 g of crown ether per solution. Neither value was at the extreme of the tested range, which was an unexpected result.

Shor’s Algorithm — A Study

A study of Shor’s integer-factorization algorithm, from its number-theoretic foundations to its quantum components. We explain the reduction of factoring to order finding, introduce the necessary linear algebra and quantum-computing concepts, and describe quantum period finding using the Fourier transform.

Scholar ↗
Summary (expand) for Shor’s Algorithm — A Study

Abstract

We explore a quantum algorithm for integer factorization known as Shor's algorithm, which, at least in theory, outperforms even the fastest known classical algorithm in terms of time complexity. In the first part, we explain the classical component of the algorithm, which is based on the fundamentals of number theory. In the second part, we tackle the quantum component, examining the foundations of quantum mechanics and quantum computing formulated around linear algebra.

Future is Written in Qubits

2022

Aleksander Kalacun (mentors: Mitja Osojnik and Marko Jagodič) · 56th Meeting of Young Researchers of Slovenia

A survey of quantum-computing foundations and applications in optimization, simulation, machine learning, and cryptography. The work includes Qiskit implementations, comparisons with classical methods, and selected experiments on IBM quantum hardware.

Publication ↗
Summary (expand) for Future is Written in Qubits

Abstract

Using quantum computer simulations and experiments on real quantum computers, we investigated problems for which quantum computing promises advances in scientific research. We compared quantum and classical approaches in terms of speed and solution quality. The selected problems represented four areas in which quantum computing is expected to have a major impact.

Our results generally agreed with published studies, but we also observed unexpected differences. In the matrix-based machine-learning example, the hybrid algorithm was almost as fast and efficient as a working, though nonoptimal, classical implementation. In our experiments with Shor’s algorithm, the quantum simulation was much slower, particularly as the problem became more difficult. We found that quantum simulations, although still far from efficient quantum computation, can help investigate problems with potentially significant effects on scientific disciplines relevant to everyday life.

How Many Fractions Are There? (Koliko je ulomkov?)

2020

Aleksander Kalacun and Črt Cigale (mentor: Jožef Senekovič) · 54th Meeting of Young Researchers of Slovenia · Murska Sobota, Slovenia

A mathematics project on converting repeating decimals to fractions and classifying fractions by the length of their repeating decimal period. Submitted to the Young Researchers of Slovenia competition, where it received a Gold Award.

Publication ↗
Summary (expand) for How Many Fractions Are There? (Koliko je ulomkov?)

Abstract

There are infinitely many natural numbers, and every rational number can be written as a fraction. A rational number also has a decimal representation that either terminates or eventually repeats. In this research paper, we investigate how to express a repeating decimal as a fraction and how this representation relates to the number of digits in the repeating block. We explore the number of fractions with a chosen period length, including the case of a single-digit repeating period.
